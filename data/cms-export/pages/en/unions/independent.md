**You can form a union without joining an international. Some workers have. Know what you'd be giving up.**

## What this means for you

An independent union is exactly what it sounds like: a union with no national parent. Trader Joe's United [A77] and Blue Bottle Independent Union [A78] are the best-known recent examples — workers who won elections and bargain contracts under their own names.

What you gain:

- **Total control.** Your dues, your priorities, your contract. No national officer can overrule your store.
- **Your identity.** The union is the crew, not a brand.

What you give up — and this list is long:

- **No strike fund.** A national union pays striking members hundreds a week. Independents can't. A strike comes out of your savings.
- **No staff lawyers or researchers.** National unions have contract negotiators, economists, and lawyers on payroll. You'll be negotiating against the company's professionals with volunteers.
- **No organizer playbook.** Nationals have organized thousands of workplaces. You're writing the playbook yourself.
- **Slower contracts.** First contracts are hard for everyone; they're harder alone.

## What to do now

1. Before choosing independent, talk to [the unions in your industry](/unions/profiles) — many now offer "minority unionism" support or lighter-touch affiliation.
2. If you still want independent, call [EWOC](/unions/no-union-yet). They coach self-organizing workers for free, including groups going independent.
3. Ask the independents themselves. Trader Joe's United and Blue Bottle Union both publish how they work and help other shops.

## Watch out

Independence is the hard mode. The workers who made it work had a strong committee, deep savings, and real experience — and they'll tell you the same. Don't choose it because a national asked for money or moved slow; choose it with eyes open.

**Pressure comes in a ladder. Each rung is more visible, more collective, and harder for the boss to ignore. Climb one rung at a time — and mean every one.**

## What this means for you

The ladder, roughly bottom to top:

1. **Buttons and shirts.** Everyone wears the same thing one day. Cost: zero. Risk: near zero. Message: we exist, we're united.
2. **Petitions and group complaints.** Twenty names, one demand, delivered in person to the manager. See [small fights first](/organize/actions).
3. **Practice pickets and rally support.** Lunch-hour pickets outside the shop, allies from other unions joining. Legal and loud — but the boss notices.
4. **Work-to-rule.** Everyone follows every rule to the letter. No unpaid minute early, no task outside the job description. Production drops, discipline is impossible.
5. **Practice strike / one-day walkout.** A short, organized stoppage that proves you can do the real thing. Huge jump in stakes — only when turnout is proven.
6. **The real strike.** The last rung. See [strike basics](/contract/strikes) first.

The pattern: each rung rehearses the next, and each one teaches the boss — and your own members — that the next is real.

## What to do now

1. **Announce the ladder as a plan.** "If there's no contract by March 1, here's what happens next" — published, dated, democratic.
2. **Take a vote before each rung above #3.** The vote IS the mobilization: people who vote for an action show up for it.
3. **Escalate when stalled, not when angry.** The ladder is a calendar, not a mood.
4. **Debrief every rung.** Who turned out, who didn't, why. Fix the gap before the next rung.

## Watch out

The biggest escalation risk isn't the boss — it's burnout and overreach. An action you can't repeat is worse than one you never did. And from rung 5 up, the legal ground changes (see [strikes](/contract/strikes)) — your union organizer should be involved in the plan.

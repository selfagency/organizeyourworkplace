**"What are your top issues?" produces a wish list. Trade-off questions produce bargaining power.**

## What this means for you

Labor Notes' guidance for contract surveys: ask questions that force priorities, and track who responded [A4]. "What do you want in the contract?" gets you everything — which means you'll fight for nothing. "If you could split a dollar between wages and retirement, how would you split it?" gets you a mandate.

The survey has a second job most committees miss: **it's an organizing contact program.** Every response is a member you can talk to, and the response rate by department tells you where your union is strong and where it's paper-thin [A4].

## What to do now

1. **Keep it short.** 10–15 questions, 5 minutes. Paper and online (a self-hosted form — see the survey tool).
2. **Ask trade-offs, not wish lists:** split-a-dollar questions, rank-these-five questions, "would you trade X for Y" questions.
3. **Track the response rate by department and shift** [A4]. Below 60%? That's not a survey problem — that's an organizing problem. Go back to [one-on-ones](/organize/first-conversation).
4. **Ask demographic-free logistics:** department, shift, how long you've worked here. That's what makes the results bargaining ammunition.
5. **Report back.** "Here's what 200 of us said" is a visible win in itself.

## Watch out

Don't let the survey substitute for the committee. A 90% response rate with no leaders identified is a good form and a weak union. The survey feeds the [demands](/contract/demands); the [committee](/organize/committee) feeds everything else.

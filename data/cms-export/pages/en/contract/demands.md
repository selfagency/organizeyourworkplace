**A demand isn't what people said they want. It's a specific, winnable, numbered proposal backed by the people who asked for it.**

## What this means for you

Survey says: pay, scheduling, respect. That's not demands — that's topics. A demand sounds like: "Minimum wage of $22/hour in year one, $23.50 in year two." "A 14-day advance schedule with no schedule changes less than 72 hours before a shift." "Just-cause discipline with a written warning ladder."

Specificity is power because it's checkable. "Competitive wages" is whatever the boss says it is. "$22" is either on the table or it isn't.

## What to do now

1. **Group survey answers into 4–6 articles:** wages, scheduling, health and safety, discipline and job security, benefits. Don't bargain 40 demands — bargain 6 hard.
2. **Put numbers on everything.** Use the [contract comparison tool](/contract/compare) to see what contracts in your industry actually say — that's what "realistic" means, not what the boss claims.
3. **Rank by support AND by leverage.** A demand 95% of members care about is worth more than a niche win. A demand that costs the company little but means everything to the floor is a trade chip.
4. **Match a person to each demand.** "This article is why Maria's department shows up to every rally." Abstract demands don't mobilize; named ones do.
5. **Keep a priority ladder:** must-haves, want-to-haves, trade chips. Never trade a must-have for a trade chip in the first session.

## Watch out

Don't confuse the bargaining agenda with the campaign agenda. You'll fight publicly for 2–3 headline demands and negotiate everything at the table. Pick headline demands that poll well — they're your mobilization engine.

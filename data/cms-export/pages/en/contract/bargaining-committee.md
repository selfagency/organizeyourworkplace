**The people at the table are the committee, but the committee is not the people at the table. The union is the whole membership.**

## What this means for you

A bargaining committee is 3–7 workers: one or two who sit at the table, the rest who keep the floor organized while bargaining runs. Pick for trust and reach, not eloquence — the best bargainer is often the quiet cook everyone believes.

Who's at the table doesn't matter as much as who's behind it. Management counts heads; the committee's job is making sure the head count is visible.

## What to do now

1. **Size it to the shop:** roughly one committee member per 20–30 workers, covering every department and shift — see [build the committee](/organize/committee).
2. **Split the roles:** table team (talks), floor team (updates, actions, turnout), comms (writes the update after every session).
3. **Report back after every session.** Same day, all members, plain language: what was proposed, what the boss said, what's next. Members who hear news from the committee act; members who hear it from rumors don't.
4. **Keep the survey engine running** [A4] — new hires, low-response departments. The committee that only talks to supporters shrinks between sessions.
5. **Train a bench.** Bargaining takes months; people burn out, transfer, get fired. Every role needs a named backup.

## Watch out

Don't let the union's paid staff negotiator replace your committee. Staff bring expertise; members bring power. A table with professionals only is a table the boss will run.

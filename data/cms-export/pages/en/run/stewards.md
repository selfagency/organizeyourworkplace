**The steward is the contract's walking enforcement arm. Every department needs one, and every worker needs to know their name.**

## What this means for you

A **union steward** is a coworker — not a paid official — trained to handle contract problems on the floor: answering questions, sitting in on meetings, filing grievances, organizing members around issues. Good steward coverage is what makes a contract feel real between bargaining rounds.

**Weingarten rights** are the legal backbone of the job: a worker who is **represented by a union** has the right to request union representation at an investigatory interview the employee reasonably believes could lead to discipline [A86]. If the request is made, the employer must grant it or cancel the interview. The representative may advise and actively assist during the interview [A86].

Note the boundary: under current law, the Weingarten right belongs to union-represented employees — unrepresented workers don't have it yet, though the NLRB could restore it in a future case [A86].

## What to do now

1. **Recruit stewards per department and shift** — trusted workers, not the loudest. Train them: contract basics, grievance steps, Weingarten script.
2. **Teach every member the script:** *"If this conversation could lead to discipline, I request my union representative."* Then stop talking. See the [wallet card](/toolkit/wallet-card).
3. **Publish who your stewards are** — names, departments, how to reach them. A union nobody can find isn't a union.
4. **Log every Weingarten denial.** Proceeding with the interview despite a valid request is illegal [A86] — see [file a charge](/help/file-a-charge).
5. **Meet stewards monthly.** Patterns across departments (write-ups spiking, schedule games) are campaign issues, not one-offs.

## Watch out

The Weingarten request must come from the employee — stewards can't invoke it for someone who stays silent. And it applies to investigatory interviews, not to ordinary work instructions or results already announced.

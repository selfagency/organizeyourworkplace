**Your boss can force you to sit and listen to an anti-union pitch at work. federally, that's still legal. Prepare for it like a storm you know is coming.**

## What this means for you

During a campaign, employers routinely hold mandatory meetings where workers are lectured on the dangers of unions — often by professional anti-union consultants. Under federal law, this remains allowed: the NLRB tried to ban mandatory anti-union meetings in 2022, but that effort was blocked in court and the prohibition is on hold as of this page's date [A44]. A dozen states have passed their own bans; several are being challenged. Check [state laws](/rights/state-laws) for yours — and assume the meeting happens anyway.

What is **not** legal, even in a captive meeting:

- **Threats** of job loss or closure for union support.
- **Promises** of benefits to reward a "no" vote.
- **Interrogation** about who supports the union.
- **Surveillance** of union activity.

That's the TIPS rule — see [what the boss can't do](/rights/what-the-boss-cant-do).

## What to do now

1. **Before the meeting**, tell your coworkers what it is: a scripted sales pitch with the company's money behind it. People who know the genre don't get converted by it.
2. **During**, listen. Take silent notes in your head or afterward on your own phone. Note the time, who ran it, exact quotes — especially any threat or promise.
3. **You may speak up.** Asking one polite, factual question ("How many of the closed stores the consultant mentioned had a contract?") can break the spell for everyone watching.
4. **You cannot be disciplined for not agreeing.** Silent skepticism is safe and effective.
5. **Afterwards**, debrief your committee and log the quotes. Threats or promises go straight toward a [ULP charge](/help/file-a-charge).

## Watch out

Don't disrupt the meeting or turn it into a shouting match — that hands the boss footage for his narrative. Your job in the room is to observe and survive it with credibility.

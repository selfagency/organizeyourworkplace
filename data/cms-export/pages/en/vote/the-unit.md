**The unit is the list of people who get to vote and, if you win, bargain together. Bosses fight over it because its shape decides who has the power.**

## What this means for you

A bargaining unit is the group of employees who share a **community of interest** — similar work, skills, pay, working conditions, supervision. The petition proposes a unit; the employer can dispute it at the hearing; the NLRB Regional Director decides [A70].

Who is generally **in**: production workers, service workers, drivers, cashiers, clerical staff — anyone doing the core work, full-time or part-time.

Who is generally **out**, under federal law:

- **Supervisors** — anyone with real authority to hire, fire, or discipline, or to seriously direct others' work using independent judgment.
- **Independent contractors.**
- **Confidential employees** with access to labor-relations secrets (like the HR person who plans the anti-union campaign).

Why the boss fights: a unit split into fragments is weaker. "Let's just include the supervisors" sounds generous — it's a poison pill, because supervisors legally owe the company their loyalty and their presence can sink the vote.

## What to do now

1. **Map the workplace with unit eyes.** Who actually disciplines whom? Who sets schedules? Your [workplace map](/organize/map-your-workplace) is the evidence base.
2. **Propose the unit that matches the work**, not the org chart. Don't let the boss's titles confuse the picture.
3. **Let your organizer and the hearing do the fighting.** Unit disputes are legal arguments [A70] — your job is the facts of who does what.

## Watch out

If someone you organized turns out to be a supervisor, they can't vote. That's not a betrayal — it's the law, and it's one more reason to know your shop's real power structure early.
